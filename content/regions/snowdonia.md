## Snowdonia (Eryri)

Right then, *cariad*, so you're thinking about Eryri? Let's have a proper chat about it — not the brochure version, the one a mate who's lived under these mountains would give you.

**In a Nutshell:** Snowdonia — *Eryri*, "land of eagles" — is Wales' biggest national park, 823 square miles of mountains, slate, and lakes. Yr Wyddfa (Snowdon) at 1,085m gets the headlines, but it's one of *fifteen* Welsh peaks over 3,000ft, and the wilder ranges — the Glyderau, Carneddau, Rhinogs, Cadair Idris — are where the park actually opens up. It's also one of only 18 International Dark Sky Reserves in the world, and the slate landscape that surrounds Blaenau Ffestiniog carries UNESCO recognition. There's more to this place than the path up Snowdon.

**Who Comes Here:** Honestly, everyone. Six million visitors a year. Hardcore mountaineers training for the Alps. Families queueing for the Snowdon Mountain Railway. Mountain bikers shredding Antur Stiniog. Stargazers driving in for clear-sky weekends at Llyn Geirionydd. Birthday-trip groups bouncing inside a Victorian slate cavern. The point is: the park flexes, but the higher and weirder the terrain you head into, the thinner the crowds get.

**What It's Best For:**
*   **Mountain hiking and scrambling.** The Welsh 3000s (all 15 peaks over 3,000ft in 24 hours) is the ultimate test, but most people start on Yr Wyddfa via the Llanberis or Pyg Path. Crib Goch is the infamous knife-edge — Grade 1 scramble, no safety net, people die here most years. Tryfan's North Ridge is the other classic. For solitude, the Nantlle Ridge or the Rhinogs deliver mountain days with a fraction of Snowdon's footfall.
*   **Rock climbing.** This is the birthplace of British climbing — Joe Brown and Don Whillans cut their teeth in Llanberis Pass at Dinas Cromlech and Dinas Mot in the 1950s. Classic trad routes still draw the pilgrimage: Cenotaph Corner, Cemetery Gates, the Idwal Slabs, Tremadog's Vector, and Cloggy (Clogwyn Du'r Arddu) above Llyn Cwellyn. Plas y Brenin in Capel Curig is the National Outdoor Centre and runs courses for everything from beginner trad to winter mountaineering.
*   **Slate-quarry adventure.** This is the unique-to-Snowdonia stuff. Zip World runs Velocity 2 at Penrhyn Quarry — Europe's fastest zip line, 100mph over a flooded slate pit — plus Titan at Llechwedd, Quarry Karts, and the Bounce Below trampoline network strung 176-year-old caverns. Go Below run guided underground trips through the abandoned Cwmorthin and Croesor mines. None of it exists anywhere else in the UK.
*   **Mountain biking.** Coed y Brenin near Dolgellau was the UK's first purpose-built MTB centre — MBR, Dragon's Back, Tarw and Cyflym trails through ancient forest. Antur Stiniog runs serious uplift-served downhill in Blaenau. Penmachno does hand-cut natural singletrack, Marin Trail at Gwydir Forest does old-school cross-country. Don't expect Bike Park Wales here — that's Merthyr, different drainage basin, different region.
*   **Whitewater kayaking.** Afon Conwy, Llugwy and Glaslyn rip after rain — grade 2 to 5 depending on the section and the gauge. Plas y Brenin runs courses. Bala (Llyn Tegid) is the flat-water alternative.
*   **Heritage railways.** Four of them: the Snowdon Mountain Railway (Victorian rack railway, Llanberis to near the summit), Ffestiniog (narrow-gauge steam, Porthmadog to Blaenau Ffestiniog through the slate landscape), Welsh Highland (Caernarfon to Porthmadog via Beddgelert), and Talyllyn (the original preserved railway, near Tywyn).

**Dark Skies:** Eryri is an International Dark Sky Reserve — one of 18 worldwide. The protected zone covers most of the high ground and a chunk of the coast. Best stargazing pull-offs: Llyn Geirionydd, Llynnau Cregennen above Dolgellau, and the Crimea Pass between Betws-y-Coed and Blaenau. New moon, dry air, late autumn or early winter. Bring layers; the temperature drops fast once the sun's down.

**Slate Country:** The valley running from Llanberis through Blaenau Ffestiniog and down to the coast is a UNESCO World Heritage Site for the slate industry that once roofed the British Empire. Dinorwig Quarry above Llanberis — the world's second-largest slate quarry — is now an atmospheric ghost town you can walk for free. Llechwedd, Cwmorthin and Penrhyn are all live tourist sites built on quarry heritage. If you only do the mountain bit, you've seen half the park.

**What It's NOT Good For:** Coasteering — there's no Snowdonia coastline worth the name; for that, go round to the Llŷn Peninsula or Anglesey. Long sandy beach days are technically possible (Black Rock Sands, Harlech, Barmouth) but better as a rest-day pairing than a holiday focus. And if you're after silence and solitude on the popular routes, you'll struggle in summer — go midweek, go early, or go to the Rhinogs.

**Weather Reality:** Snowdonia generates its own weather and the mountain conditions can be completely different from the valley you left an hour ago. Always check the **MWIS** (Mountain Weather Information Service) and **Met Office Mountain Forecast** — both linked in the practical info on every activity page. Summit windchill in summer can sit at 5°C with the valley at 22°C. Above 600m in winter, snow is normal; above 800m the avalanche risk is real and crampons are not optional. Phone signal is patchy in valleys and dead on most peaks — download offline OS maps before you set off.

**Getting Here & Around:** Driving is functionally essential — the A55 is the main artery from the north, the A470 from Cardiff. Trains reach Bangor (3.5h from London Euston), Llandudno and Betws-y-Coed. The **Snowdon Sherpa** (S1/S2) buses connect Llanberis, Pen-y-Pass, Beddgelert and the main trailheads — hourly on summer weekends. Pen-y-Pass car park (£10) fills by 7am in season; the Sherpa is the smarter play.

**Welsh Language:** Eryri is one of the strongest Welsh-speaking heartlands left — around 60% of locals in Gwynedd speak Cymraeg as a first language. You'll hear it in the shops in Llanberis, the cafés in Blaenau, the pubs in Capel Curig. Place names mean things: *cwm* (valley), *llyn* (lake), *bwlch* (pass), *afon* (river). Learning *sut mae?* ("how are you?", pronounced *sih-my*) and *diolch* ("thanks") goes a long way — people often switch to English with a smile. The Welsh names aren't decorative; they're how the landscape is actually read.

**Best Time to Visit:** May–June and September are the sweet spot — long days, fewer crowds, full facilities. July–August is warmest but the park is rammed. Autumn (late September into early November) brings gold in Gwydir Forest and the cleanest stargazing nights. Winter rewards experienced mountaineers; everyone else should stick to lower walks and the heritage railways. Avoid school holidays if you can help it.

**The Smart Play:**
- *One day:* hike Yr Wyddfa via the Pyg Track from Pen-y-Pass (arrive on the 6:30am Sherpa), descend via the Miners' Track. Pint at the Heights in Llanberis afterwards.
- *A weekend:* day one as above. Day two: bike Coed y Brenin (Beics Brenin do hire on-site), or — if it's raining — Go Below underground at Cwmorthin and a steam train back from Blaenau.
- *A week:* mix it up. Two mountain days (one Yr Wyddfa route, one Tryfan or the Glyderau). One slate day (Llechwedd or Dinorwig). One bike day (Coed y Brenin or Antur Stiniog). One coast day on the Llŷn or to Harlech. One rest day in Beddgelert eating Glaslyn ice cream and reading by a lake. One clear-sky night somewhere dark.

*Iawn?* Don't try to do it all. The park rewards the trips that respect the scale of it. Enjoy.
